## 0:30 – 1:15 · The problem and the aim

**On screen:** the NVTV public archive listing — long programmes with a title and
sometimes a synopsis, and nothing else.

> Northern Visions Television is a community broadcaster in Belfast. Its public
> archive holds long-form recordings of the city's civic life — protests, launches,
> panel discussions, blue-plaque unveilings. Each item has a title. Nothing describes
> what happens *inside* it. To find one moment, an archivist has to watch the whole
> programme.
>
> Automatic description is the obvious remedy, and foundation models make it
> technically easy. But a single-model pipeline publishes every field with the same
> apparent confidence, whether three models agreed or one model guessed. For an
> archive that matters: a missing field can be worked around, a confidently wrong
> field silently corrupts the catalogue. This project had no budget for manual
> annotation, so the usual route to a gold standard was closed.
>
> The aim, then: produce segment-level metadata using only pretrained models, and
> attach to every published field an auditable estimate of how strongly independent
> sources support it.

## 1:15 – 2:15 · Input data and segmentation

**On screen:** the `VIDEO FILES` folder, then `01_metadata_pipeline.ipynb` running the clip
stage, then the clips folder filling, then `clip_manifest.csv`.

> This is the input: 47 programmes, all from 2016, five hours and five minutes of
> video in total. ⏸
>
> The first stage cuts each programme into fixed 30-second clips with FFmpeg, using stream copy so
> nothing is re-encoded. Every clip is FFprobe-verified after writing, and each worker renames on
> success, so an interrupted run cannot leave a truncated file behind.
>
> That produces 626 clips with zero failures. ⏸ The manifest records every clip's
> source, its start time, a SHA-256 of the source file, and its split. The split is
> assigned at *source* level with a fixed seed, so no programme contributes clips to
> both the calibration and evaluation sets — 123 clips for calibration, 503 for
> evaluation.

---

## 2:15 – 3:30 · Generating the five fields

**On screen:** `01_metadata_pipeline.ipynb`, the metadata stage; then Table II of the paper.

> Each clip is then annotated across five fields, and this is the central design
> decision: every field is generated by **at least two architecturally unrelated
> model families**. ⏸
>
> Transcript comes from two Whisper checkpoints. On-screen text from Tesseract and EasyOCR — one
> a classical line recogniser, the other a neural detection stack. Keywords from KeyBERT, YAKE and
> TF-IDF. Visual tags from two CLIP variants against a 34-label vocabulary. And people count from
> BLIP visual question answering, ViLT, and DETR — a captioning model, a transformer, and a
> convolutional detector: three unrelated routes to one integer.
>
> The reason for that diversity is not accuracy. It is that these models fail in
> *different* ways. CLIP makes ranking errors. A VQA model has answer priors. A
> detector misses small objects. Because the mistakes are uncorrelated, agreement
> between them carries information — in a way that agreement between two checkpoints
> of the same architecture does not.

---

## 3:30 – 4:30 · The consensus stage

**On screen:** a published JSON record, zoomed so the `status`, `models`,
`agreement_score` and `needs_caution` fields are clearly legible.

> Here is what the system actually publishes. ⏸ This is one field of one clip.
>
> It carries the value, an evidence status — observed, not detected, conflict, or
> error — the list of models that supported it, an agreement score, and a caution
> flag.
>
> Three rules make that score honest. First, self-similarity is excluded, so a source
> never corroborates itself. Second, empty output is never promoted: if every source
> agrees that nothing is present, the field is recorded as not-detected, never as high
> agreement — without that rule a corpus of quiet, low-text clips would report perfect
> reliability. Third, on-screen text has a hard veto: a string is published only if
> both OCR engines find it, it persists across at least two frames, and the two
> engines agree at 0.80 similarity. A hallucinated name in a catalogue is the most
> damaging error available here, so that field is deliberately conservative.

## 4:30 – 5:15 · How a user uses it

**On screen:** the Flask review dashboard. Play a clip, scroll its record, point at a
high-agreement field and then at one carrying `needs_caution`.

> This is the reviewer's view. The clip plays on the left, its record sits on the
> right. ⏸
>
> The value of this is triage. This field is supported by four independent families
> and is safe to publish. ⏸ This one is flagged for caution — one source, no
> corroboration, and a human should look at it before it enters the catalogue.
>
> That is the deliverable: not a list, but a *triaged* list. It tells an archivist
> where to spend their attention, and it can be produced with no annotation budget at
> all.

---

## 5:15 – 5:45 · Training — and why there is none

**On screen:** Table III of the paper, the configuration surface.

> A word on training, because it is the obvious question. **Nothing in this system is
> trained or fine-tuned.** Every model runs zero-shot at its published checkpoint.
> That is deliberate: an archive with no annotated reference cannot supervise
> training, and a system whose product is a reliability estimate must not be tuned
> against labels it does not have.
>
> What replaces training is this configuration surface — sampling policy, OCR
> thresholds, agreement tiers — and every one of those values was chosen on the
> 123-clip calibration split. The evaluation split was never used for any selection
> decision.

---

## 5:45 – 6:30 · How the system is evaluated

**On screen:** `02_vlm_benchmark.ipynb` resuming from its prediction caches, then
`03_vlm_benchmark.ipynb` rebuilding the comparison tables.

> Evaluation is split across two notebooks, and the split is deliberate. ⏸
>
> `02_vlm_benchmark.ipynb` runs the seven vision-language models over every clip under one
> protocol — four frames, one prompt, greedy decoding, the same 34-label vocabulary. It writes
> one prediction cache per model and it is fully resumable: a re-run loads the cache, retries
> only the records that failed, and skips everything already done. An interrupted GPU job costs
> nothing. Each cache also carries a prompt version, so a cache written against an older prompt
> is ignored rather than silently mixed into current results. ⏸
>
> `03_vlm_benchmark.ipynb` loads no models at all and needs no GPU. It reads the ground truth and
> those caches and produces every comparison table in seconds. That separation is the single
> highest-value structural decision in the project: scoring can be corrected and re-run without
> paying for inference again.

---

## 6:30 – 7:30 · What the evaluation found

**On screen:** the four figures from the paper in turn.

> Five experiments evaluate the system. ⏸
>
> The headline result is this. **Agreement ranks fields by how independent their
> sources are, not by how hard the task is.** People count, with four unrelated
> families, reaches 0.812. Keywords reach 0.173 — because all three extractors read
> the same Whisper transcript. Three views of one input are not three sources of
> evidence, and the system's own numbers expose that. ⏸
>
> On-screen text conflicts on 53.4% of clips and reaches high agreement on none — the veto working
> as specified, with its cost now measured. ⏸
>
> Robustness is field-specific, not system-wide. Under blur, visual tagging holds 0.96
> of its output while on-screen text retains 0.07 — a thirteen-fold difference under
> one perturbation. ⏸
>
> The hosted annotator refuted my own hypothesis: it agrees with the local consensus
> only 42% of the time, yet rewrites 49% of published tag sets. ⏸
>
> And across the seven benchmark models, the *smallest* — InternVL3-2B — leads three of five fields
> and beats its own 8B sibling. Scale did not predict agreement. Eight of eleven things I expected
> turned out to be wrong, and those are the findings worth having.

---

## 7:30 – 8:10 · Limitations and future work

**On screen:** the limitations section of the paper.

> The limitations are real and stated plainly. There is no human reference, so every
> number measures corroboration, never accuracy. Two ablations are underpowered, at
> eight and five clips. Two of my source pairs are correlated, which inflates their
> fields. And corroboration is blind to upstream error — if speech recognition fails,
> all three keyword extractors agree on the same wrong text.
>
> The most valuable next step is small: a few hundred human-annotated clips would
> convert every agreement figure in this project into a measure of validity. Beyond
> that — per-source reliability weights, an audio-capable benchmark model, shot-aware
> segmentation, and speaker diarisation.
