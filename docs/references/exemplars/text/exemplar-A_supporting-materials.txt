1. Extended Literature and Context 
 
1.1 Scope and Method 
The research paper's literature review is limited to approximately one page and is 
focused on the work that directly supports the contribution. The purpose of this section 
is to expand that review across five areas: blade inspection datasets and detection 
techniques, conventional detection architectures, vision foundation models, foundation 
models in industrial inspection, and vision-language approaches to anomaly detection. 
Sources were identified using Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore and ScienceDirect. The term 
combinations used were wind turbine blade, defect detection, UAV inspection, 
foundation model, vision transformer and anomaly detection, supplemented by forward 
and backward citation tracing from the papers cited in the research paper. Priority was 
given to peer-reviewed work published from 2021 onwards, with earlier work included 
only where it establishes context that later work builds upon. 
 
1.2 Blade Inspection: Data, Methods and Problems 
Automated blade inspection research divides into two areas: work that provides data 
and work that provides architecture. These two areas have developed at different rates, 
and the resulting imbalance between them is the field's defining characteristic. 
Dataset contributions. Shihavuddin et al. [1] produced the DTU-Drone dataset, which 
remains the most widely used resource in automated blade inspection. Four variants of 
Faster R-CNN were tested on it, with the best achieving 81.1% mAP using Inception-
ResNet-V2. The influence of DTU-Drone is far greater than its size would suggest, largely 
because so few alternatives exist. Foster et al. [2] continued detection work using drone 
footage from the same domain. Gohar et al. [3] subsequently addressed the dataset's 
greatest limitation by releasing a set of standardised five-class annotations, and later 
proposed slice-aided hyper-inference for analysing ultra-high-resolution imagery [4]. 
Yang et al. [5] developed Blade30, a dataset focused on image stitching rather than 
detection. More recently, Ji et al. [6] released WTBD with six defect categories and a 
defined split protocol, and Zhang et al. [7] released WTBs2025 spanning nine categories. 
Architectural contributions. A large body of literature focuses on modifying the YOLO 
family for blade inspection. Yu et al. [8] combined CBAM attention and BiFPN within 
YOLOv8. Zou et al. [9] developed DCW-YOLO for surface damage detection. He et al. [10] 
proposed an adaptive multi-scale detection method. Li et al. [11] modified YOLOv5 for 
operational condition monitoring from UAV imagery. Guo et al. [12] applied deep 
convolutional networks to blade damage identification, and Li et al. [13] combined an 
improved SegFormer with pixel matching to measure defect size. Dwivedi et al. [14] 
addressed blade and solar panel defects jointly using an attention-based model. 
Alternative sensing modalities. Some researchers have pursued better data rather than 
better architectures. Jia and Chen [15] combined optical and thermal video for near-real-
time segmentation. Memari et al. [16] combined multi-spectral RGB and thermal imaging 
to detect anomalies on small turbines. Rizk et al. [17] analysed hyperspectral imaging 
data using 3D convolutional networks. Deng et al. [18] took the opposite approach and 
applied classical digital image processing. 

The unaddressed problem. Three shared aims run through this literature. Firstly, almost 
every study evaluates against a single dataset, so how well a model generalises across 
sources is rarely examined. Secondly, each dataset uses a different taxonomy, which 
makes merging difficult and cross-study comparison unreliable; Gohar et al. [19] 
highlights this issue specifically in their review. Thirdly, and least acknowledged, 
annotation quality is assumed rather than verified. None of the studies investigated here 
reported checking the accuracy of the annotations they trained on. The annotation audit 
carried out during this project found that two categories within a dataset published as a 
high-quality image dataset contained significant labelling errors, which indicates that 
this assumption is not always safe and that annotation verification should become 
standard practice in the field. 
The architectural improvements proposed in this literature: deeper backbones, attention 
modules, sliced inference, all address data scarcity by modifying the model. None 
considers whether the source of the inputted data could be altered instead, and that 
question forms the basis of the present study. 
 
1.3 Conventional Detection Architectures 
The single-stage detection family provides the baselines used in this work. The YOLO 
family has converged on an anchor-free, decoupled-head design: YOLOv8 [20] 
established the modern form, YOLOv9 [21] introduced adjustable gradient information to 
address information loss in deeper networks, and YOLOv10 [22] removed the non-
maximum suppression stage to allow end-to-end training. RT-DETR [23] takes a different 
route, adapting the DETR set-prediction formulation for real-time reasoning and reporting 
higher COCO accuracy than comparable YOLO variants. 
All four are designed for object detection in general, where training data is plentiful and 
objects occupy a substantial portion of the frame. Both assumptions do not hold for 
blade inspection. Datasets are small, and defects are frequently only a few tens of pixels 
across within an image several thousand pixels wide. This mismatch motivates the 
small-object techniques common in the domain literature, and it is also the condition 
under which a strong, pretrained representation would be expected to provide the 
greatest benefit. 
 
1.4 Vision Foundation Models and Plain-Backbone Detection 
Two developments make the present approach possible. 
Plain backbones for detection. Li et al. [24] showed that a non-hierarchical vision 
transformer can be used as a detection backbone and only requires a simple feature 
pyramid constructed from a single-scale feature map rather than a network specifically 
designed for detection. This breaks the link between backbone quality and detection-
specific design and is the architectural example for the model described here. 
Agglomerative pretraining. Self-supervised and language-supervised pretraining have 
produced several strong but specialised backbones. CLIP [25] learns a joint vision-
language representation with strong zero-shot classification performance. DINO [26] and 
its successor DINOv2 [27] generate robust patch-level embeddings that transfer well to 
dense prediction. SAM [28] provides fine-grained segmentation ability. Ranzinger et al. 
[29] recognised that these capabilities are complementary and refined all three into AM-
RADIO, an agglomerative student model that outperforms each of its teachers across 
most benchmarks while retaining both zero-shot and dense-prediction behaviour. 
Heinrich et al. [30] refined this further with RADIOv2.5, using multi-resolution training 

and balanced teacher losses to address resolution-dependent mode switching and 
teacher imbalance. 
The agglomerative approach is particularly suited to defect detection because the task 
has a dual nature. Locating small defect regions is a dense localisation problem, best 
served by DINOv2-style features, while distinguishing between visually similar defect 
types is a semantic discrimination problem, best served by CLIP-style features. A single-
teacher backbone would without doubt trade one against the other. 
 
1.5 Foundation Models in Industrial Inspection 
The application of foundation models to industrial defect detection has developed 
largely within the anomaly detection space, which differs from the present work in an 
important respect, it treats defects as deviations from a learned idea of ‘normal’ rather 
than as classes to be detected. 
Memory-bank methods. SPADE [31] and PaDiM [32] introduced multi-scale patch 
correspondence and Gaussian modelling of patch features. PatchCore [33] combined 
these ideas into a widely adopted approach: extract dense patch embeddings from a 
pretrained backbone, compress them through coreset selection, and score anomalies by 
nearest-neighbour distance. GraphCore [34] extended this with graph-based memory. 
These methods are appealing because they require no training, but Reiss et al. [35] 
showed that their performance depends heavily on the quality of the underlying feature 
encoder. 
Foundation-model backbones. That dependence motivated substituting stronger 
encoders. AnomalyDINO [36] builds a patch-memory pipeline on DINOv2 and achieves 
state-of-the-art few-shot performance on MVTec-AD [37] with competitive results on 
VisA [38]. RadioCore [39] extends the idea to an agglomerative backbone, using 
RADIOv2.5 features in a multi-scale memory bank with k-nearest-neighbour matching. 
Iqbal et al. [40] pursued a reconstruction-based alternative using multi-scale feature 
reconstruction. 
Distinction from the present work. All these methods are training-free: the backbone is 
used as a fixed feature extractor and no gradients pass through it. The current work fine-
tunes the backbone alongside a trainable detection head, which is a categorically 
different proposition. The benchmarks used throughout, bottles, capsules, printed 
circuit boards, also resemble web-image pretraining distributions far more closely than 
aerial imagery of weathered composite surfaces, so the domain shift these methods 
face is considerably smaller than the one examined here. 
 
1.6 Vision-Language Models for Defect Description 
The secondary aspect of this project draws on similar literature applying vision-language 
models to industrial inspection. WinCLIP [41] showed that CLIP embeddings enable 
zero-shot anomaly classification through a structural ensemble of attribute words and 
prompt structures, combined with window-based feature aggregation for localisation. 
APRIL-GAN [42] and MuSc [43] developed related zero-shot approaches, while 
AnomalyCLIP [44] proposed object-agnostic prompt learning to reduce the category-
specific engineering each new prompt otherwise requires. Zajec et al. [45] studied the 
corresponding few-shot problem in manufacturing. 
All these studies report strong performance on manufactured-object benchmarks, but 
all were conducted solely within that domain. The replication presented in Section IV-E 
of the research paper shows that the reported performance does not transfer to aerial 

blade imagery, which is consistent with the domain-shift argument above and supports it 
directly. 
 
1.7 Synthesis and Gap 
Three concerns come from this review. Blade inspection research is primarily concerned 
with architectural refinement, and few studies address whether a model generalises 
across sources or whether the annotations used to train it are accurate. Vision 
foundation models have shown somewhat promising results in industrial inspection, but 
almost exclusively in training-free settings where domain shift between source and target 
is limited. No study combines the two approaches: fine-tuning an agglomerative 
foundation model with a trainable detection head for blade defect detection and 
comparing its performance against contemporary conventional detectors under both 
single-dataset and merged cross-dataset training. 
The research paper addresses that combined gap, which is shown to be a genuine one 
through this review. 
 
 
2. Project Life Cycle 
 
2.1 Overview and Methodology 
The project followed an iterative, experimental cycle rather than a straightforward 
waterfall. The output of each stage shaped the design of the next: initial baseline results 
determined the hyperparameter search space, per-dataset results motivated the cross-
dataset design, and measurement faults discovered during evaluation forced revisions to 
the training pipeline itself. This section describes each stage and notes where the plan 
changed and why. 
Supervision took the form of regular progress updates against the agreed objectives. Two 
supervisory interventions shaped the work materially. Early in the project, it was 
established that the work should demonstrate whether foundation models can 
outperform smaller conventional models when appropriately fine-tuned. Later, a 
requirement was set that this comparison be conducted in both per-dataset and merged 
cross-dataset settings, supported by lots of experimentation. 
 
2.2 Planning and Scoping 
The initial scope established the research question, identified AM-RADIO as the 
candidate foundation model, and selected the YOLO and RT-DETR families as the 
conventional comparison. Three decisions taken at this stage influenced everything that 
followed. 
Choice of backbone. AM-RADIO was chosen over single-teacher models such as 
DINOv2 because blade defect detection requires both dense localisation and semantic 
discrimination between visually similar categories. The full rationale is given in Section 
5.1. 
Choice of task framing. The problem was framed as supervised object detection rather 
than unsupervised anomaly detection, which is the more common framing for 
foundation-model work in industrial inspection (Section 1.5). Supervised framing was 
chosen because the available datasets carry class labels, and because the model needs 
defects categorised rather than just flagged. 

Choice of comparison protocol. Benchmarking against eighteen conventional 
architectures rather than one was decided early, to prevent the result being attributed to 
a poorly selected baseline. 
Three risks were identified at planning stage: limited availability of annotated blade 
datasets, computational constraints associated with a 651-million-parameter backbone, 
and the absence of a common taxonomy across sources. All three happened. The first 
and third initially proved more damaging than imagined. The second was managed 
successfully but shaped the experimental design throughout. 
 
2.3 Data Acquisition and Preparation 
Five datasets met the criteria for use: Blade30, the Gohar re-annotation of DTU-Drone, 
WTBs2025, WTBD, and Yae, derived from the dataset of Liu and Liu. Initially, only three 
datasets were found (Blade30, Gohar and WTBD), however the lack of data made 
generalisation extremely difficult, especially in certain individual classes which were 
severely underrepresented. WTBs2025 and Yae were then found to fill that gap.  
Preparing these datasets required considerably more effort than predicted. Each arrived 
in a different annotation format and used a different defect taxonomy, ranging from two 
to nine categories with no consistency in naming. A single six-category taxonomy was 
therefore defined, and a single conversion pipeline written to transform all five sources 
into it. Using one pipeline rather than per-dataset scripts was intended to ensure that 
conversion differences could not introduce contradicting factors into the cross-dataset 
comparison. 
The greatest unanticipated effort at this stage was the annotation audit described in 
Section 3.4. Examining a subset of annotated instances revealed that one dataset 
contained substantial annotation error, which required manual review of 1,085 images in 
a single category and the exclusion of seven of its nine categories. This work was not 
planned for, and it consumed a significant portion of the schedule, but it prevented 
training on unreliable labels, underrepresented classes and produced a finding reported 
in the research paper. 
 
2.4 Implementation 
Implementation worked across three components. 
Conventional baselines. Built using the Ultralytics framework with minimal custom 
code, starting from official COCO-pretrained checkpoints. The framework's original 
training loop, augmentation and evaluation were left unmodified, so that the baseline 
reflects what a developer would realistically achieve rather than a deliberately weakened 
comparison. 
Foundation-model detector. Written as custom code. It comprises a wrapper exposing 
AM-RADIO ViT-H/16 features, three trainable projection blocks constructing a P3/P4/P5 
pyramid, and the Ultralytics FPN/PAN neck and Detect head reused so that the two 
families differ only in backbone and adaptation strategy. A configurable freezing 
mechanism controls how many final transformer blocks receive gradients. 
Vision-language pipeline. Developed independently. Cropped regions were extracted 
from all five datasets with padding and a minimum dimension threshold, negative 
examples were sampled from image corners, the resulting examples were converted to 
an instruction format, and LoRA fine-tuning was performed using Unsloth. 
Development was iterative rather than specified-then-built. Several architectural 
decisions, GroupNorm in place of BatchNorm, the EMA implementation, and gradient 
clipping thresholds, were made in response to observed training behaviour rather than 

selected in advance. Section 5.2 gives the reasoning behind these choices and 
considers, with hindsight, which could have been anticipated. 
 
2.5 Experimentation 
Experimentation ran in two arms. 
Per-dataset arm. For each of the five datasets, eighteen conventional architectures 
were trained and the best retained, and a nine-configuration grid search was run over the 
two hyperparameters controlling backbone adaptation. This produced 45 foundation-
model training runs and 90 baseline runs. 
Cross-dataset arm. The training splits of four datasets were pooled into a single 4,243-
image set, with Blade30 excluded entirely. Both families were trained on the pooled data 
and evaluated on the merged validation split and on each source's individual test set. 
Compute management. All parts ran on Google Colab using a single NVIDIA A100-SXM4 
40GB GPU. Each merged-dataset epoch took approximately 7.5 minutes, with an 
evaluation pass every ten epochs adding a further 14 minutes, so a 100-epoch run 
required roughly 13 hours. Checkpoints were written to Google Drive at ten-epoch 
intervals so that a session disconnection would not lose the run. This cost shaped the 
experimental design directly: the cross-dataset configuration was carried forward from 
per-dataset results rather than independently re-searched, because a nine-configuration 
grid at merged scale was not affordable. 
 
2.6 Evaluation and Iteration 
Evaluation was a continuous process rather than a final phase. Three separate faults 
were identified during this stage, each requiring a return to implementation. 
The first was an exponential moving average evaluation lag, caused by the custom EMA 
implementation not matching the framework's methodology, which understated 
performance by 0.29 mAP at one checkpoint. The second was batch normalisation 
instability at batch size 4, producing a mean absolute discrepancy of 0.28 to 0.38 
between training and evaluation behaviour and an apparent mid-training collapse; this 
was resolved by replacing BatchNorm with GroupNorm. The third was a checkpoint 
serialisation fault, in which a saved model failed to reproduce the validation score 
recorded in its own training log. This was detected by reloading the checkpoint and re-
evaluating it rather than relying on the logged score alone. 
The third fault required the merged experiment to be re-run in full. The re-run 
incorporated the GroupNorm change together with a verification step and produced a 
checkpoint that reproduced its logged metric to within 0.003 mAP. 
 
 
3. Verification and Validation 
 
3.1 Approach 
Verification was conducted at five levels: data origin, label correctness, data quality, the 
training process, and saved artefacts. The methodology was only partially planned. It 
mainly developed in response to faults identified during the project, each of which was 
only detectable by testing something that had previously been assumed correct. The 
resulting principle, that any value the pipeline reports should be independently 
reproducible from the artefacts it produces, is the project's main methodological lesson 
and is discussed further in Section 5.3. 
 

3.2 Data Provenance and Cross-Dataset Contamination 
Because the cross-dataset experiment trains on pooled data and evaluates on individual 
test sets, an image appearing in more than one source would contaminate the 
evaluation. Several blade datasets derive from overlapping UAV collections, so this was 
a tangible risk rather than a theoretical one. 
Every image across all five datasets was hashed using the imagehash library. The 
resulting hashes were compared pairwise between datasets at a distance threshold 
permitting both exact matches and near-duplicates. Matches were recorded with their 
distance values and reviewed individually, sorted by confidence. Confirmed duplicates 
were quarantined rather than deleted, so that the decisions taken remain auditable. 
Perceptual hashing is established practice elsewhere in computer vision, but it does not 
appear in the blade inspection literature surveyed in Section 1. Omitting it would leave 
any cross-dataset generalisation claim vulnerable to undetected overlap between 
sources. 
 
3.3 Label Correctness 
Two independent methods were used. 
Round-trip provenance verification. Rather than inspecting converted labels in 
isolation, each converted image was matched back to its raw source using its perceptual 
hash. The correct class set was then extracted directly from the raw annotation file, in 
LabelMe JSON format for Blade30, Pascal VOC XML for WTBD and Yae, and per-folder 
YOLO labels for WTBs2025, and compared against the classes written by the conversion 
pipeline. For every dataset, the procedure reports how many images were checked, how 
many could not be matched to a raw source, and every class mismatch with both the 
expected and written values. 
This process was carried out across all splits for Blade30, Yae and WTBs2025. It 
validates the conversion pipeline against ground truth rather than against itself and 
detects both conversion faults and errors in the source annotations. Where mismatches 
appeared, each instance was traced back to its raw source and examined to establish 
whether the mismatches followed a systematic pattern. 
Class index mapping. Index-to-class mappings were verified against the metadata 
distributed with each dataset, including Roboflow classes.txt and data.yaml files where 
present, rather than being inferred from folder names. Silent index misalignment 
produces a model that trains successfully and evaluates meaninglessly, causing failure 
with no visible symptoms. 
Visual class sampling. Labelled instances were rendered per class in grid form for each 
dataset, so that categories could be inspected for internal consistency and for 
agreement with the shared taxonomy. 
 
3.4 Data Quality and the WTBs2025 Audit 
Visual inspection revealed that one dataset's annotations disagreed with its image 
content at a rate that made it unusable as released. 
WTBs2025 spans nine defect categories across 7,544 images. Three categories had no 
equivalent in the shared taxonomy and were excluded at the outset. The lightning strike 
category was excluded because it combines 57 real photographs with 228 GAN-
synthesised images that could not be reliably separated, a limitation that the dataset's 
own authors document. The remaining categories were checked visually, and sampled 
crack and erosion annotations were found to be incorrect at rates of approximately 80% 

and 40% respectively, in the sense that the labelled region did not contain the stated 
defect. Both categories were therefore excluded. 
Of the remaining categories, only coating detachment could be retained, and then only 
after exhaustive manual review. An interactive review tool was written to display each 
candidate image alongside its annotation for acceptance or rejection, with files pre-
filtered to those containing the target class. All 1,085 images were reviewed individually 
and 161 were approved. The approved filename list was written to storage so that the 
decision set is reproducible, rather than dependent on a particular notebook session. 
Contamination, which sampling found reliable at approximately 90%, was kept entirely. 
Two categories and 933 images were kept from nine categories and 7,544 images. This is 
a significant rejection rate for a dataset published as a high-quality resource, and it 
demonstrates clearly that annotation verification should form part of any research effort 
using image-based datasets rather than overreliance on the validity of the source. 
 
3.5 Training Process Verification 
Model selection parity. The custom EMA implementation used in the foundation-model 
pipeline initially differed from the framework's approach, producing a value 0.29 mAP 
lower at one checkpoint. It was revised to use the same decay rate and time constant as 
the framework, so that both families select checkpoints under equal criteria. 
Normalisation stability. Backbone memory constrains the batch size to four. At that 
size, batch normalisation accumulates unstable running statistics. Measuring the 
difference between training-mode and evaluation-mode behaviour showed a mean 
absolute discrepancy of 0.28 to 0.38, which produced an apparent mid-training collapse 
that was a measurement artefact rather than a genuine model failure. Replacing batch 
normalisation with group normalisation eliminated the discrepancy. 
Gradient behaviour. Pre-clipping gradient norms were measured and found to range 
between approximately 100 and 500 against a clipping threshold of 10.0, indicating that 
clipping was active on virtually every iteration. This was confirmed at both batch size 4 
and batch size 8, which establishes that per-step update magnitude is determined by 
clipping regardless of batch size and therefore bounds the practical impact of the 
effective batch size asymmetry between the two pipelines. 
Configuration isolation. An earlier version of the freezing mechanism set parameters to 
trainable without resetting them, so the number of unfrozen blocks accumulated across 
configurations sharing a common backbone object. The revised mechanism returns the 
backbone to a fully frozen state before each configuration is run. 
 
3.6 Artefact Verification 
This level was added in due to a failure that none of the previous checks would have 
detected. 
A merged dataset run reported a best validation score of 0.8306 mAP@0.50, with a 
consistent learning curve across 100 epochs. When the saved checkpoint was then 
loaded for per-test-set evaluation, it produced 0.0438, with a maximum class confidence 
of 0.097 on annotated defect images, comparable to its response to randomly generated 
input. The training log and the saved weights described different models. 
The problem was found by elimination. State dictionary keys matched completely, 
normalisation buffers were finite and correctly built up, box decoding was confirmed 
against the raw output format, and the run's per-epoch history was recovered and 
confirmed to show genuine learning. The fault was found to be checkpoint selection and 

serialisation under the batch normalisation architecture that run had used, which has 
since been replaced. 
The fix was to implement a verification step into the training loop. After each checkpoint 
is written, the file is reloaded into a fresh model, re-evaluated, and the reloaded score 
compared against the logged score. The re-run reproduced its logged metric to within 
0.003 mAP, with raw and EMA weights agreeing to within 0.0001 at convergence. 
The lesson is that a training loop's reported metric is an assertion about an in-memory 
object rather than about the file on disk. Equivalence between the two must be 
demonstrated, not assumed. 
 
3.7 Evaluation Protocol Validation 
Both model families were trained to their own convergence under identical early-
stopping criteria rather than a fixed epoch budget, evaluated at the same intersection-
over-union thresholds through the same metric implementation, and selected on the 
same criterion. 
Performance is reported at two IoU thresholds rather than one. A plain vision transformer 
backbone and a purpose-designed convolutional detector might differ in localisation 
quality specifically, and reporting both thresholds makes any such difference visible 
rather than concealing it behind a single figure. 
WTBD provides no independent test split, so its validation split was used for evaluation 
throughout. Its results are therefore less strictly held out than those of the other four 
datasets. 
Two aspects of the evaluation remain unvalidated. All results are produced from single 
training runs per configuration, and checkpoint-to-checkpoint variation reached 0.15 
mAP on the smaller datasets, which exceeds the margin recorded on the smallest 
dataset; multi-seed evaluation would be required to establish that particular result with 
confidence. In addition, the merged conventional baseline was a single architecture 
rather than the best of the eighteen-architecture sweep used per dataset. 
 
 
4. Tools Used to Support Development 
 
4.1 Compute and Execution Environment 
Google Colab (NVIDIA A100-SXM4 40GB). All training and evaluation were performed in 
this environment. Colab was chosen because a 651-million-parameter backbone at 
1024×1024 input exceeds the memory capacity of a typical consumer GPU, and it 
provided A100 access without requiring formal cluster provisioning. 
Its limitations affected the project more than those of any other tool. Sessions 
disconnect without warning, so every long-running process required checkpoint logic. 
Local storage under /content is purged on disconnection, so datasets had to be re-
copied. A 100-epoch merged-dataset run required approximately 13 hours, at 7.5 
minutes per epoch plus a 14-minute evaluation pass every ten epochs. This cost 
restricted the experimental design directly: the cross-dataset hyperparameter 
configuration was carried forward from per-dataset results rather than searched from 
scratch, because a nine-configuration grid at merged scale was not affordable. 
Three mitigations were implemented in response. Training loops check whether a local 
dataset copy exists and re-copy it automatically from Drive if not, rather than allowing 
the framework to fall back silently to a default dataset, which would produce 
meaningless results without raising an error. Completed runs are recorded in a JSON log 

and skipped on re-execution. Partially completed runs are detected by counting rows in 
the framework's results.csv and resumed from the last checkpoint rather than restarted 
from scratch. 
Google Drive. Persistent storage for datasets, checkpoints and results, mounted into the 
Colab runtime. It was chosen for durability across sessions rather than for performance. 
 
4.2 Detection Frameworks 
Ultralytics. Used to establish the baseline results and partly, the foundation-model. It 
provided the eighteen architectures benchmarked (YOLOv8n/s/m/l, YOLOv9c/e/s/m, 
YOLOv10n/s/m/l, YOLO11n/s/m/l, RT-DETR-l/x), each from official COCO-pretrained 
checkpoints, together with its native training loop, augmentation, evaluation and non-
maximum suppression. 
Ultralytics was selected for two reasons. It is the standard choice for a developer to 
employ, so the baseline provides a genuine comparison rather than an artificially weak 
one. Its C3k2, Conv and Detect modules could also be reused within the foundation-
model architecture, so that the two families share detection logic and differ only in 
backbone and adaptation strategy, which is the difference being studied. 
One framework-specific adjustment was necessary. RT-DETR required different 
hyperparameters from the YOLO family, using a classification loss weight of 1.2 rather 
than 2.0 and an initial learning rate of 1x10⁻⁵ rather than 1x10⁻², showing its different loss 
formulation. Using default YOLO everywhere would have created an incorrect show of 
weakness in the strongest transformer-based baseline. 
A key limitation is that Ultralytics accumulates gradients to a nominal batch size of 64 by 
default, whereas the custom foundation-model pipeline does not. This inconsistency is 
mentioned in the research paper. 
PyTorch. Used directly for the custom foundation-model training loop, the freezing 
mechanism, the exponential moving average, mixed-precision training with gradient 
scaling, and checkpoint serialisation. 
torch.hub. Used to retrieve AM-RADIO ViT-H/16 checkpoints, selected because it is the 
distribution channel the model's authors provide. 
torchmetrics. Used to calculate mean average precision within the foundation-model 
pipeline, providing an application independent of Ultralytics so that both families are 
evaluated through a comparable metric. 
 
4.3 Data Preparation and Verification 
imagehash. Perceptual hashing, used for two purposes: detecting duplicate and near-
duplicate images across datasets, which would otherwise contaminate cross-dataset 
evaluation, and matching converted images back to their raw sources so that label 
correctness could be verified against the original annotations. The second use is what 
made the round-trip verification described in Section 3.3 possible. Exact file hashing was 
considered as an alternative, but re-encoded or resized copies of the same image 
produce different byte-level hashes while retaining very similar perceptual hashes. 
ipywidgets. Used to build the interactive review tool for the WTBs2025 coating 
detachment audit, displaying each candidate image with its annotation for acceptance 
or rejection. Reviewing all 1,085 images would have been impractical without such a 
tool, which also wrote the approved filename list to storage so that the decision set is 
reproducible rather than confined to a single session. 

PIL and matplotlib. Image loading, rendering annotations over source images for visual 
verification, per-class sample grids, training curve plots, and the qualitative detection 
figure. 
PyYAML, pandas and shutil. Dataset configuration files; reading framework log files to 
determine resumption points; and dataset copying with retry logic, since Drive 
operations occasionally fail quickly under load. 
 
4.4 Vision-Language Tooling 
Hugging Face Transformers. Model loading and inference for BLIP-2, in both OPT and 
FlanT5-XL configurations, and Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct during zero-shot evaluation. 
OpenCLIP (LAION-400M). Used to replicate WinCLIP with the same backbone and 
compositional prompt ensemble described in the original publication, so that the 
replication tests the published method rather than a variation. 
Unsloth with LoRA. Used for supervised fine-tuning of Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct, updating 
around 1.1% of its 2.15 billion parameters. This approach was chosen because full fine-
tuning of a 2B-parameter vision-language model would have exceeded the memory 
available, while Unsloth's FastVisionModel provides memory-efficient training through a 
straightforward interface. 
scikit-learn. Used to compute AUROC for the WinCLIP replication, consistent with the 
evaluation methodology of the original publication. 
This section required its own session recovery mechanism. Adapters were written to 
drive immediately after training, and a reload path was implemented so that a 
disconnection mid-evaluation did not require retraining. 
 
4.5 Development and Version Control 
Jupyter and Colab notebooks. Development was organised into separate notebooks by 
stage: dataset conversion, dataset verification, per-dataset baseline evaluation, per-
dataset foundation-model training, cross-dataset experimentation, and vision-language 
experimentation. Separating them kept the long-running training notebooks isolated 
from the exploratory verification work, so that a crash in one did not affect the others. 
One significant disadvantage of the notebook format is that state continues implicitly 
between cells, so a variable defined in a cell that has since been deleted remains live in 
the session. This contributed directly to the difficulty of diagnosing the checkpoint fault 
described in Section 3.6, where an evaluation cell referenced a model class defined in a 
deleted cell. Moving code into version-controlled modules imported by the notebooks 
would remove this class of problem entirely. 
GitLab. Version control and submission. 
 
4.6 Summary of Tool-Driven Constraints 
Three constraints that lead back to tooling shaped the results reported in the research 
paper. Colab's session limits and per-epoch cost determined that the cross-dataset 
configuration was carried forward rather than re-searched. Backbone memory at 
1024×1024 input constrained the batch size to four, which caused the batch 
normalisation instability that motivated the switch to group normalisation. And 
Ultralytics' default gradient accumulation, absent from the custom pipeline, produced 
the effective batch size asymmetry disclosed as a limitation. 
 
 
5. Critical Appraisal 

 
5.1 Divergence from the Original Specification 
The delivered project differs from the original specification in four respects. Each 
divergence was deliberate, and each is justified below. 
Backbone: DINOv3 to AM-RADIO. The specification proposed DINOv3. AM-RADIO was 
substituted following discussion with the supervisor, on the grounds that defect 
detection requires two capabilities simultaneously that no single-teacher backbone 
supplies. DINOv3 yields strong structural patch representations, but blade defect 
detection also requires precise boundary delineation, such as tracing the perimeter of a 
fracture or the irregular edge of coating loss, along with semantic discrimination 
between visually similar categories. An agglomerative model distils multiple specialist 
teachers into a single encoder: SAM contributes segmentation-quality boundary 
sensitivity, DINOv2 contributes dense structural features, and CLIP contributes open-
vocabulary semantic grounding. Since the published evidence is that the agglomerative 
student exceeds each individual teacher on most benchmarks [29], the substitution is 
not a compromise between them but an improvement over any one of them for a task 
requiring all three. 
Output: visual encoder to detector. The specification originally described the 
deliverable as a strong visual encoder. This was reframed during implementation 
because a visual encoder alone cannot perform the task the project sets out to evaluate. 
AM-RADIO alone emits abstract feature representations and has no concept of a 
bounding box. Producing something capable of locating and classifying defects required 
constructing an entirely separate architecture on top of it: three trainable projection 
blocks mapping constant-resolution patch features into a P3/P4/P5 pyramid, a feature 
pyramid and path aggregation network, and a detection head with its associated loss. 
That construction was not optional, and it is not deviation from the specification's intent 
but the necessary consequence of evaluating an encoder on a detection task. 
Modality: ultra-high-resolution video to still images. The specification proposed fine-
grained analysis of ultra-high-resolution drone video. This proved infeasible within the 
project's compute and data constraints, for reasons that became clear working with just 
images. 
A single 1024×1024 forward pass through a 651-million-parameter backbone 
constrained the batch size to four on a 40GB A100, and a 100-epoch run on 4,243 images 
required approximately 13 hours. Video at inspection-grade resolution multiplies this 
cost in three ways: frame count, since even short inspection sequences contain 
thousands of frames; resolution, since ultra-high-resolution footage would require tiled 
inference rather than a single downsampled pass; and temporal modelling, which would 
add architecture and parameters beyond the per-frame detector. Beyond compute, no 
annotated blade inspection video dataset with frame-level defect boxes was identified 
during the literature search described in Section 1 and constructing one would have 
consumed the entire project. 
Training strategy: hybrid supervised and self-supervised to purely supervised. The 
specification proposed a hybrid strategy implementing dense visual supervision and 
synthetic data, whereas the delivered work is purely supervised. Two factors drove this. 
Firstly, the datasets acquired carry class labels and bounding boxes, so supervised 
training uses the available signal directly. Secondly, and more inevitably, the annotation 
audit described in Section 3.4 consumed a substantial portion of the schedule, and 
adding a self-supervised pretraining stage and synthetic data generation on top would 
have left insufficient time for the experimental comparison identified as the project's 

core requirement. The decision was to establish a rigorous supervised comparison 
rather than a partial hybrid one. 
Assessment of the divergences. The first two are justified on technical grounds and 
would have been the right choices regardless of scheduling. The third is forced by 
resource constraints that were not anticipated. The fourth is a reduction in scope linked 
to unplanned data quality work. Taken together, the delivered project answers a tighter 
question than the specification proposed, but answers it with rigour that a broader scope 
would have precluded. 
 
5.2 Design and Implementation Rationale 
Plain-backbone detection architecture. The projection pyramid follows the approach 
of Li et al. [24], who established that a non-hierarchical vision transformer works as a 
detection backbone with only a simple feature pyramid built from a single-scale feature 
map. This was chosen over remaking a hierarchical backbone because it isolates the 
variable being inspected. 
Reuse of the Ultralytics neck and head. Sharing the C3k2, Conv and Detect modules 
between families ensures that the only differences between them lie in the backbone 
and the adaptation strategy. Developing a tailored detection head would have introduced 
a second uncontrolled variable. 
COCO-pretrained baselines. Both families start from large-scale pretraining. Training 
the baselines from random initialisation would have produced a larger apparent margin 
and an invalid comparison, as the study concerns the pretraining corpus and the 
adaptation strategy rather than the presence or absence of pretraining. 
Per-architecture baseline tuning. RT-DETR required different hyperparameters from the 
YOLO family because of its different loss formulation. Applying YOLO defaults to it would 
have created a false impression of weakness in the strongest transformer-based 
baseline. 
GroupNorm in place of BatchNorm. Adopted to address instability measured during 
experimentation rather than chosen in advance, as described in Section 3.5. 
Hyperparameter search design. Two parameters were varied: unfrozen block depth and 
the backbone-to-head learning rate ratio. Input resolution, augmentation policy, 
schedule length and clipping threshold were held constant across the grid so that 
differences could be attributed cleanly to the two parameters under investigation. A 
broader search space could not be explored given the per-epoch cost documented in 
Section 4.1. 
 
5.3 Lessons Learned 
Measurement faults resemble model faults. Each of the three issues encountered, 
EMA evaluation lag, BatchNorm instability, and the serialisation fault, initially presented 
as poor model performance. Time was spent investigating model architecture and 
hyperparameters before the measurement path was suspected. The core takeaway is to 
verify and track the metric before attempting to optimise it. 
Published data is not verified data. WTBs2025 is published in a peer-reviewed data 
journal under a title describing it as a high-quality dataset, yet two of its categories 
contain substantial labelling error. Had this not been checked, results would have been 
reported on partly meaningless labels with no indication that anything was wrong. 
Notebook state is a hazard. The implicit retention of state across cells contributed to 
the difficulty of diagnosing the checkpoint fault, where an evaluation cell referenced a 
model class defined in a cell that had since been deleted. In the future, moving stable 

code into version-controlled modules imported by the notebooks would eliminate this 
kind of problem. 
 
5.4 Evaluation of the Outcome 
What was achieved. A methodologically matched comparison across five datasets, 
eighteen conventional architectures and forty-five foundation-model configurations, in 
both per-dataset and merged cross-dataset settings. A data-volume threshold identified 
and supported by two independent lines of evidence. A cross-dataset result in which the 
foundation model outperforms the conventional baseline on every individual test set. A 
verification methodology that identified substantial annotation error in a published 
dataset. And three measurement faults found and corrected rather than reported 
through. 
What the results support. The threshold finding is the most transferable outcome: 
foundation-model adaptation in this domain requires a minimum annotation volume, 
below which a smaller architecture fine-tuned from general pretraining is the better 
choice. The cross-dataset result supports a secondary claim that broad pretraining 
allows tolerance of source heterogeneity. 
What the results do not support. Single-seed reporting means the margin on the 
smallest dataset sits within observed checkpoint variance and should be read as 
indicative rather than established. The merged baseline was a single architecture rather 
than the full sweep, so the merged margin is not measured against an equivalently tuned 
comparison. And with only one backbone evaluated, it is not established whether the 
threshold is a property of AM-RADIO specifically or of foundation-model fine-tuning for 
detection more generally. 
What would be done differently. Per-epoch cost would be measured before scope was 
committed to. Checkpoint serialisation would be verified from the first run rather than 
the last. Annotation verification would be budgeted for explicitly rather than discovered 
as a necessity. And compute would be reserved for multi-seed replication of the headline 
result, which would have converted the strongest finding from indicative to established.
