Abstract — Large language models show substantial 
zero-shot information-extraction (IE) capability, yet 
conversational text — informal, multi-turn, and 
context-dependent — remains poorly served by IE 
systems designed for static, formally structured 
documents. This paper reports an experimental study 
into adapting an open-source 8-billion-parameter 
model, Qwen3-8B, for structured extraction from 
clinical doctor-patient dialogue, using a custom 798-
example benchmark built from the MTS-Dialog 
dataset. Four research questions are examined: 
whether 
parameter-efficient 
LoRA 
fine-tuning 
outperforms zero- and few-shot prompting (RQ1); 
whether explicit annotation guidelines embedded in 
the fine-tuning prompt improve generalisation to 
unseen clinical specialties (RQ2); which of three 
structured 
output 
representations 
minimises 
hallucination (RQ3); and how extraction performance 
degrades with conversational complexity (RQ4). Naive 
fine-tuning on 250 examples gave null results relative 
to prompting (macro-F1 0.757 vs. 0.776 few-shot), 
whereas guideline-conditioned fine-tuning yielded a 
modest, real improvement (0.780) with reduced 
hallucination. JSON output minimised hallucination 
relative to natural-language and Python-dataclass 
methods, and a measurable but partly artefactual 
generalisation gap was observed between seen and 
unseen specialties. Following supervisor-approved 
scope narrowing to a single clinical domain, the 
resulting benchmark, evaluation harness, and findings 
are presented as a foundation for future development.

I. 
INTRODUCTION 
A. Problem and Context 
Information extraction (IE) is the transformation 
of unstructured text into structured, machine-
readable records; it has traditionally assumed static, 
well-formed input: news articles, scientific abstracts, 
legal filings. Conversational data violates every one 
of these assumptions. Statements are informal, 
incomplete, spread across multiple turns between 
speakers and mostly rely on implicit context (e.g. a 
symptom mentioned three turns earlier referenced 
only as “it”). This gap matters more as organisations 
accumulate large volumes of conversational data — 
clinical encounters, customer-service transcripts and 
voice-assistant logs — that must be mined for 
structured, actionable content. 
Large language models have demonstrated 
substantial zero-shot IE capability, but this capability 
is neither free nor uniform. GPT-3.5 achieves only 
18.22 F1 on standard named-entity recognition 
without task-specific adaptation [3], and zero-shot 
dialogue-state tracking trails fine-tuned models by 
10–20 points on MultiWOZ [6], a benchmark built 
on the neural dialogue-systems paradigm established 
by earlier pretrained-language-model approaches [5]. 
Existing benchmarks rarely assess entity, relation, 
intent, and event extraction jointly within multi-turn 
conversational context, and this gap is sharpest in 
high-risk domains such as healthcare, where 
extraction errors carry real consequences. 
B. Research Aim and Objectives 
The aim of this project is to determine how an 
open-source LLM can be accurately adapted, through 
fine-tuning, prompt design, and output-format 
selection, to extract structured clinical facts from 
doctor-patient conversation, and to characterise the 
conditions under which each adaptation method 
succeeds or fails. This splits into four objectives, 
each mapped to one research question addressed in 
Section IV:  
I. 
Establish whether parameter-efficient fine 
tuning improves on prompting alone. 
II. 
Establish 
whether 
explicit 
annotation 
guidelines 
improve 
generalisation 
to 
specialties unseen during training  
III. 
Establish 
which 
structured-output 
representation best balances accuracy 
against hallucination. 
IV. 
Characterise 
how 
extraction 
quality 
becomes 
worse 
with 
conversational 
complexity. 
The expected contributions are threefold: a 
conversational IE benchmark for the clinical domain; 
a reusable system for scoring three output formats 
against identical gold labels; and an honest empirical 
account of where small-scale fine-tuning performs 
both above and below prompting. 
C. Scope and Boundaries 
Following a supervisor-approved scope review, 
the study is confined to healthcare conversations 
within the MTS-Dialog corpus and does not extend 

to a multi-domain benchmark, proprietary-model 
baselines (GPT-3.5/4), or reproduction of the 
baseline 
approaches 
(InstructUIE, 
GoLLIE, 
FnCTOD) originally envisaged. A fifth research 
question, on joint multi-format generalisation, was 
also no longer considered, in line with the project's 
risk-mitigation plan. As a direct consequence of this 
narrowed scope, the findings reported here should be 
read as specific to a single clinical domain rather than 
as general claims about conversational information 
extraction. 
D. Research Questions 
RQ1: Does parameter -efficient fine tuning (LoRA) 
outperform zero-shot and few-shot prompting for 
conversational information extraction? 
RQ2: Does embedding explicit annotation guidelines 
in the fine-tuning prompt improve zero-shot 
generalisation to clinical specialties unseen during 
training? 
RQ3: Does a JSON function-calling output 
representation reduce hallucination and improve 
structural validity relative to natural-language labels 
and python data class instances? 
RQ4: How does extraction performance degrade 
across levels of conversational complexity and 
between seen and unseen specialities? 
E. Methodology Overview and Paper Organisation. 
An experimental machine-learning methodology is 
followed across four phases: benchmark creation, 
model adaptation, evaluation and error analysis. The 
key literature behind this methodology is outlined in 
Section 2. Details about the data set model 
architecture setup, and the outcomes of RQ1 to RQ 
4 are described in Section 3. The conclusions and 
limitations of this research are covered in Section 5. 
II. 
LITERATURE REVIEW 
Four papers form the core literature basis for 
this project, jointly informing its treatment of fine-
tuning versus prompting, guideline-conditioned 
training, 
and 
structured-output-format 
design, 
though none combines all three within a 
conversational, clinical setting. 
On-Demand Information Extraction (ODIE) [1] 
fine-tunes LLaMA-7B with LoRA on a ChatGPT-
generated instruction-extraction dataset, pulling 
user-specified content into structured tables from 
free text. ODIE outperforms its baseline by nearly 
5% and approaches GPT-4 despite being far smaller, 
showing that a fine-tuned open model can compete 
with a proprietary one on structured extraction. Its 
six-step data-generation pipeline is the direct 
template this project adapts for benchmark 
construction. Its limitation is that it operates only on 
static documents, leaving multi-turn context 
resolution and speaker coreference unaddressed. 
Zero-shot dialogue-state tracking via function 
calling (FnCTOD) [2] represents each dialogue 
domain as a callable function whose arguments are 
the user-specified slot values; a fine-tuned 
FnCTOD-LLaMA2-13B 
matches 
ChatGPT’s 
tracking performance. Its JSON function-call format 
is the direct precedent for this project’s JSON output 
condition (RQ3). Its limitation is a closed, fixed slot 
schema that cannot express the open-ended free-text 
fields clinical extraction requires. 
InstructUIE [3] unifies named-entity, relation, 
and event extraction under a single instruction-tuned 
FlanT5-11B model and generalises to seven 
domains unseen during its own training, motivating 
this project’s seen/unseen specialty split (RQ4). Its 
natural-language output format is the basis for this 
project’s “NL” condition. It reports no hallucination 
metric; this project’s RQ3 finding that the NL-label 
format hallucinates far more (33.8–58.0%) than a 
JSON alternative (7.5–9.7%) directly extends its 
evaluation. 
GoLLIE [4] trains models to follow explicit 
annotation 
guidelines, 
expressed 
as 
Python 
dataclasses, combined with five regularisation 
techniques, allowing a 7B model to outperform an 
11B InstructUIE by 13 F1 points with under 1% 
hallucination. It is the direct source of this project’s 
dataclass output condition and the entire premise of 
RQ2. Its limitation relative to this project is that it is 
evaluated only on static news and biomedical text, 
using substantially more regularisation than is 
reproduced here. 
Read together, these papers reveal a clear gap: 
extraction and generalisation are studied without 
dialogue (ODIE, InstructUIE); dialogue is addressed 
only 
within 
closed, 
task-oriented 
schemas 
(FnCTOD); and guideline-conditioning with output-
format hallucination control is demonstrated only on 
static text (GoLLIE). No existing work combines a 
fine-tuning-versus-prompting 
comparison, 
guideline conditioning, and output-format control 
within a single conversational, open-ended-field 
benchmark. This project’s contribution is precisely 
that combination, evaluated in the clinical domain 
under identical conditions on identical held-out data. 
III. 
METHODS 
A. Dataset 
The benchmark was built from 800 conversations 
sampled from the MTS-Dialog dataset, a publicly 
available collection of doctor-patient dialogue 
transcripts developed for clinical note-generation 
research. Each conversation was passed through 
Qwen3-8B to extract five fields against a fixed 

schema: 
symptoms 
(name/duration/severity), 
family_history (relation/condition), medications 
(name/dose/frequency), diagnosis (free text), and 
recommendation (free text) — a mix of structured 
list fields and open free-text fields, bridging 
FnCTOD’s closed slot-value schema and ODIE’s 
open table extraction. 
The annotation prompt includes direct handling for 
two frequent error types identified during early trial 
runs. Negation: a symptom the patient clearly denies 
(e.g. “no, I haven’t”) must not be extracted as 
present, since a naive extractor otherwise records the 
symptom regardless of the surrounding denial. 
Attribution: a fact belonging to a family member or 
another person mentioned in the conversation must 
not be recorded as the patient’s own symptom, 
medication, or diagnosis. Both rules were added 
after manual inspection showed each error type 
occurring systematically rather than incidentally, 
and both are checked again, independently, at the 
self-verification stage described below. 
 
Two automatic quality filtering methods 
were tested on the raw annotations before human 
review. An NLI based regularity check, testing 
whether each extracted fact was entailed by the 
source text, was implemented and tested but 
abandoned: it proved unreliable on long, multi turn 
medical dialogue, flagging 717 of 1,687 facts, many 
of which were correct on inspection. It was replaced 
with Qwen3 self-verification pass, and the same 
model family fact-checks its own extraction against 
the source conversation and the whole human-
written summary, flagging 275 facts (16.3%) for 
review. A full human review pass then corrected 
every flagged fact, most commonly negation errors 
and one misattribution case.

B. Model Architecture 
The backbone model is Qwen3-8B, an 
open-source, 
instruction-tuned, 
decoder-only 
transformer, selected for strong reported instruction-
following at the 7–8B scale, open weights permitting 
full gradient access unavailable with GPT-3.5/4, and 
mature LoRA/PEFT tooling. The model is loaded in 
4-bit precision, a choice made necessary by the 8 GB 
VRAM laptop GPU. A LoRA adapter is attached to 
the four attention projection matrices (query, key, 
value, output) with rank 16, scaling alpha 32, and 
dropout 0.05, training 15,335,424 of 8,206,070,784 
parameters (0.187%) while leaving base weights 
frozen. Rank, alpha, and target-module selection 
follow validated LoRA and GoLLIE methods rather 
than a project-specific search; a relatively low rank 
was preferred over a larger value specifically 
because the RQ1/RQ2 training subsample contains 
only 250 examples, where a higher-capacity adapter 
would carry a greater risk of overfitting at the cost 
of some representational flexibility. 
C. Training Procedure 
Training data for the RQ1 condition consists of 250 
of 709 seen examples (fixed seed = 42), each pairing 
the extraction prompt with the JSON annotation as 
the target completion; prompt tokens are masked so 
cross-entropy loss is computed only over the target 
completion. Training uses a per-device batch size of 
1 with gradient accumulation over 8 steps, one 
epoch, learning rate 2e-4, fp16 precision, and the 
paged 8-bit Adam optimiser, with gradient 
checkpointing enabled. For RQ2, an otherwise 
identical script substitutes an alternative, guideline-
augmented prompt — the same JSON schema 
prefaced with per-field inclusion/exclusion rules — 
while every other hyperparameter is held fixed, 
isolating guideline presence as the sole independent 
variable. 
D. Hyperparameter Rationale and its Limits 
Hyperparameters were derived from established 
literature rather than a project-specific search, a 
direct consequence of the single-consumer-GPU 
compute budget: each fine-tuning and evaluation 
cycle required over an hour of GPU time, making a 
multi-point hyperparameter sweep impractical 
within the available timeframe. Two engineering 
issues were identified and fixed during the RQ2 run. 

First, longer guideline-augmented prompts caused 
71% (177/250) of training examples to exceed the 
original 640-token cap, silently truncating the target 
completion; this was corrected by compacting the 
guideline text and raising the cap to 768 tokens, 
reducing the truncation rate to 8% (20/250). Second, 
GPU memory pressure at 96–97% of the 8 GB card 
caused Windows to spill allocations into system 
RAM; switching gradient checkpointing to non-
reentrant mode gave partial relief. These constraints 
are treated as limitations rather than settled 
methodological choices.

B. Evaluation Metrics and Rationale 
 
The plan’s originally specified strict-span 
F1 metric was judged after inspection, poorly suited 
to free-form clinical text, where a correct extraction 
may take many surface forms (eg. “Kids” vs 
“Children”). Two adjusted metrics were used 
instead, applied identically across every RQ: fuzzy 
slot-level F1 (list fields: token-Jaccard overlap at a 
0.5 threshold, so paraphrases count as a match) and 
token-overlap F1 (free-text fields: precision/recall 
over predicted vs gold token sets). Macro-F1, the 
unweighted mean of the five per-field scores, is used 
as the single headline number. Hallucination rate 
(the fraction of extracted facts whose tokens do not 
appear in the source conversation) and structural 
validity (parse success rate) are reported alongside 
macro-F1 throughout, consistent with the plan’s 
focus on grounding and format reliability.

V. 
DISCUSSION 
A. Interpretation of Results 
RQ1's null result represents an important 
empirical finding, not merely a failed experiment: for 
a modern, highly instruction-tuned 8B model, 
prompting with a small number of in-context 
examples may already capture nearly all the 
performance extractable from 250 examples, and a 
small, single-epoch LoRA fine-tune does not easily 
translate that same budget into a comparable gain. 
This raises a practical question for a model user: 
whether to spend a fixed example budget on in-
context examples or on fine-tuning. The results here 
favour the former, contrary to the implicit 
assumption behind the original project proposal. 
RQ2’s modest, positive result is arguably the 
project’s most actionable finding for practitioners: 
explicit annotation guidelines, added at effectively 
zero marginal training cost — the guideline-
conditioned run uses the identical 250-example 
subsample, LoRA configuration, and training 
arguments as the no-guidelines run, with only the 
prompt text differing — yielded both higher accuracy 
and lower hallucination. Although the magnitude 
(2.3 points) falls short of GoLLIE’s own reported 13-
point gain, the direction and consistency of the effect 
(simultaneously higher F1 and lower hallucination, 
not a trade-off between the two) suggests guideline-
conditioning is a genuinely useful, low-cost lever, 
and that GoLLIE’s larger reported gain likely reflects 
its additional regularisation techniques (class-order 
shuffling, 
dropout, 
paraphrasing) 
rather 
than 
guideline text alone — a hypothesis this project’s 
simpler ablation is well positioned to have surfaced, 
precisely because it isolates guideline text as the sole 
variable. 
RQ3, clean and unambiguous across every 
metric, is the project’s strongest result, and its 
practical implication follows directly: for a 
hallucination-sensitive, high-stakes domain such as 
clinical extraction, JSON function-calling output 
should be the default choice, not merely one option 
among three. The finding that few-shot examples 
increase, rather than decrease, hallucination for the 
NL-label and dataclass formats is a genuinely novel 
observation relative to the reviewed literature and 
merits further mechanistic investigation — for 
example, whether it reflects in-context examples 
inadvertently licensing free elaboration in less-
constrained output grammars. RQ4’s finding — that 
roughly a third of the apparent seen/unseen 
generalisation gap is a scoring artefact rather than a 
genuine model failure — is a caution against over-
interpreting any single aggregate metric without 
manual error analysis, and reinforces the value of the 
fuzzy/token-overlap metric design over a stricter 
alternative that would have obscured, rather than 
revealed, this distinction. 
B. Implication and Significance 

As a whole, these results imply a clear 
recommendation 
for 
practitioners 
developing 
conversational clinical IE systems within a limited 
compute 
budget: 
prioritise 
output-format 
engineering 
(JSON) 
and 
prompt/fine-tuning 
conditioning over extensive fine-tuning effort, at 
least until a larger training-data budget is available. 
This 
conclusion 
contradicts 
the 
common 
assumption, implied throughout the reviewed 
literature, that fine-tuning is the primary mechanism 
for domain adaptation. 
C. Limitations 
• 
Monodomain: all findings are domain-
specific to clinical/healthcare dialogue from 
the MTS-Dialog dataset; generalisability to 
other 
conversational 
domains 
(e.g., 
customer 
support, 
e-commerce, 
legal 
intake), originally planned for in the project 
plan, is untested. 
• 
Small unseen split sample size: the 
RQ1/RQ2 headline comparison relies on 89 
unseen instances: an observed 1–2-point 
macro-F1 difference at such a sample size is 
not 
significant 
enough 
to 
claim 
conclusively without significance testing 
(e.g., bootstrapped confidence intervals) 
before being treated as conclusive, which 
was not done within the scope of this 
project. 
• 
Proprietary model not included: the lack of 
a GPT -3.5/4 comparison due to the lack of 
API budget hinders the open-source vs 
proprietary aspect originally envisioned in 
the plan; all model comparisons in this 
paper are done internally using Qwen. 
• 
Not reproduced against literature baselines: 
although InstructUIE, GoLLIE 7B, and 
FnCTOD-LLaMA2-13B are covered in 
detail in Section 2, none of those models 
were tested on this benchmark, due to 
hardware limitations and the descoping of 
the benchmark. 
• 
 The Annotator-Verifier correlation: both 
the annotation phase and the self-
verification 
phase 
employ 
models 
belonging to the same family, i.e., Qwen3; 
any inherent extraction tendency in such 
models is potentially imperceptible through 
self-verification as the verifier suffers from 
the same flaws as the annotator. 
• 
Search 
of 
hyperparameter 
space 
incomplete: the selection of parameters 
such as LoRA rank, LoRA alpha, learning 
rate, and epochs was made based on 
literature recommendations, and therefore, 
the negative result for RQ1 cannot be 
separated from an under-training effect 
entirely. 
• 
Human 
evaluation 
incomplete: 
an 
evaluation package consisting of 60 
questions has been developed but not yet 
implemented, and therefore, the automatic 
evaluation 
measures 
that 
were used 
throughout this paper have not yet been 
validated with human evaluation 
VI. 
CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 
CONSIDERATION 
A. Summary of Contributions 
This project aimed to establish how a publicly 
available LLM can be fine-tuned for structured 
extractions from clinical conversations, considering 
four research questions on fine -tuning, guideline 
conditioning, 
output 
format 
and 
complexity 
degradation. It presents a 798-example, human-
annotated, stratified benchmark of structured 
extraction from clinical conversation; a reusable 
evaluation harness that rates three structured output 
formats using identical gold labels; a genuine, 
positive finding in relation to guideline-conditioned 
fine-tuning: a definitive, literature-revealing finding 
for JSON output format, as well as a novel finding 
relating to few-shot examples and hallucination; and 
a stratified degradation analysis that distinguishes 
between 
genuine 
generalization 
and 
scoring 
artifacts. The RQ2 adapter was also merged into a 
standalone 
model 
(merge_lora_adapter.py) 
powering an interactive extraction demo used in the 
recorded video demonstration. 
B. Critical Discussion of Limitations 
The most significant limitation is that of scope: with 
only one clinical area, no baseline from either the 
proprietary data or the literature, and unexplored 
hyperparameters, the results here can be seen as 
specific to the domain and the particular 
configuration tested and not as generalized 
statements 
about 
fine-tuning, 
guideline 
conditioning, or output formatting broadly. The 
sample size of the RQ1/RQ2 study (n = 89) further 
implies that while the point estimates provided are 
useful in terms of direction, they have not been 
subjected to statistical significance testing and then 
remain suggestive of, but not conclusive in, their 
findings.

through 
bootstrapped

• 
Data scale and number of epochs 
ablation study to check if the negative

•

D. Unresolved Questions 
Two questions remain genuinely open: whether 
RQ1’s null result reflects a fixable data/compute 
constraint or a real ceiling effect in strongly 
instruction-tuned 
models, 
and 
the 
precise 
mechanism behind RQ3’s few-shot-driven increase 
in hallucination for less-constrained output formats. 
VII. 
AI ETHICAL CONSIDERATION 
A. Bias 
The benchmark’s specialty distribution is 
imbalanced (Table 2): general medicine accounts for 
33.3% of all examples, while the two least-
represented specialties (oncology and urology) 
account for just 2% each. Any model evaluated 
against this benchmark will therefore show 
inconsistent performance across specialties, with 
those most underrepresented in the data showing the 
worst results. This is compounded by the fact that the 
initial annotation and self-verification steps use the 
same underlying model family: a systematic bias in 
how 
that 
model 
perceives 
specialty-specific 
vocabulary risks slipping through the verification 
stage undetected. 
B. Privacy 
This corpus refers to a publicly available 
research data set that does not come from real-life 
patients. Future application to real-life clinical data 
will need to be accompanied by de-identification, 
compliance, and institutional ethics, none of which 
is in scope of the current project. 
C. Fairness 
The seen versus unseen performance 
difference is a fairness issue if such a system were 
deployed 
in 
practice, 
as 
the 
patients 
of 
underrepresented medical specialties would suffer 
from systematically inferior quality of extraction, 
without letting the doctor know about it. 
D. Transparency 
Hallucination rate is presented as a first-class metric 
throughout the paper, in the spirit of transparency: 
even the highest performing JSON output format 
still suffers from hallucination at a 7.5–9.7% rate, 
and this rate needs to be disclosed by any system 
based on the current pipeline. 
E. Accountability and Human Oversight 
Due to the hallucination rates and generalization 
error between seen and unseen cases discussed 
above, the tool is strictly used as a drafting tool for 
clinicians only with mandatory human review.
