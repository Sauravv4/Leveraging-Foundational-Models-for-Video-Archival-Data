Fine-Grained Vision Foundation Models for 
Automated Wind Turbine Inspection 
                                             
Abstract — Automated visual inspection of wind turbine 
blades for structural defects such as erosion, cracks, lightning 
strike damage, and coating delamination presents a viable 
alternative to manual survey, but existing methods typically 
involve training convolutional architectures from scratch on 
scarce domain-specific data. This work explores the potential 
of a large-scale visual foundational model (AM-RADIO), 
adapted with a lightweight detection head, to exceed the 
performance of standard detectors (YOLOv8/9/10/11, RT-
DETR) despite having no turbine-related images in its 
pretraining 
set. 
Two 
experimental 
conditions 
were 
investigated on five independently collected blade-defect 
datasets: a per-dataset scenario (train and evaluate on four 
datasets, evaluate on the same dataset) and a cross-dataset 
scenario (train on four datasets, evaluate on the fifth). AM-
RADIO outperformed the strongest of eighteen conventional 
architectures on four of five datasets by margins of 0.0218–
0.0704 mAP@0.50, but underperformed by 0.0907 on the 
smallest dataset (210 training images), and on merged cross-
dataset training reached 0.8306 mAP@0.50 against 0.785 on 
the baseline. These results show that foundation-model 
adaptation outperforms conventional detectors in this domain 
only above an annotation threshold of a few hundred images, 
and that combining varied datasets is a practical route to 
surpassing that threshold without additional labelling. 
Keywords — Detection, Foundation models, Transfer 
Learning, Generalisation, Turbines. 
I. INTRODUCTION 
Wind turbine blades operate under continuous 
mechanical and environmental stress, accumulating erosion, 
cracking, lightning-strike damage and coating loss which, if 
undetected, progress to structural failure or unscheduled 
outage [1]. Inspection currently relies on rope-access 
technicians or manual review of UAV imagery; both are 
slow, costly, and limited by inspector fatigue, motivating 
automated vision-based defect detection. 
Published approaches almost all train convolutional or 
transformer detectors either from scratch or from generic 
ImageNet initialisation, on a single small domain-specific 
dataset [2], [3]. This is a reasonable default given how few 
annotated blade datasets exist, but it leaves an alternative 
untested: foundation models pretrained on diverse imagery 
unrelated to wind energy may transfer better than any 
architecture trained on scarce in-domain data. Blade 
inspection is a well-suited testbed for this question precisely 
because every available dataset is small, defect taxonomies 
differ between sources, and the sources are rarely combined 
[4]. 
This work determines whether AM-RADIO [5], an 
agglomerative foundation model fitted with a lightweight 
detection 
head, 
outperforms 
conventional 
detectors 
(YOLOv8–v11, RT-DETR) on blade defect detection, both 
per dataset and on data pooled across sources. Four 
objectives follow: (i) establish correctly measured 
conventional baselines across five defect datasets; (ii) fine-
tune AM-RADIO per dataset, searching unfrozen backbone 
depth and backbone-to-head learning-rate ratio; (iii) merge 
the datasets and test whether foundation-model fine-tuning 
generalises more effectively than conventional detectors 
under pooled, heterogeneous data, including on a fully held-
out dataset; and (iv) report a methodologically matched 
comparison in both settings. A secondary objective 
evaluates whether vision-language models can describe 
blade defects, zero-shot and after supervised fine-tuning. 
Two questions are addressed. Q1: does AM-RADIO 
fine-tuning outperform conventional detectors when trained 
and evaluated on each dataset's own splits, and does any 
advantage hold across datasets of differing size? Q2: does 
merged cross-dataset training improve AM-RADIO's 
performance and generalisation by a margin exceeding that 
achieved by conventional detectors under the same regime? 
Both families are trained to convergence under matched 
early-stopping criteria and evaluated under a common 
protocol. The scope is five blade defect datasets, one 
foundation backbone, and a fixed set of conventional 
baselines; 
edge-hardware 
latency 
and 
alternative 
foundation-model families are outside it. 
Section II reviews related work; Section III details 
datasets, architecture, training and tuning; Section IV 
reports results; Section V discusses findings and limitations; 
Section VI concludes. 
II. RELATED WORK 
A. Blade Defect Detection 
Shihavuddin et al. [2] created the DTU-Drone UAV 
dataset and used it to train four Faster R-CNN versions to 
detect leading edge erosion, lightning receptor damage, and 
associated surface conditions. Using an Inception-ResNet-
V2 architecture, they achieved a mean average precision 
(mAP) of 81.1%. 
While the DTU-Drone dataset has been used extensively 
since its release, it was initially provided without 
standardised bounding box annotations. As a result, 
subsequent researchers have re-annotated the same 
photographs using their own taxonomic systems; therefore, 
the results of those subsequent studies cannot be directly 
compared to each other. To provide a basis for comparison, 
Gohar et al. [6] created a standardised five-class annotation 
scheme for the same images.

In addition to providing a consistent annotation scheme 
for the same set of images, Gohar et al. [7] also introduced 
slice-aided hyper inference. Slice-aided hyper-inference is a 
method of processing ultra-high-resolution drone imagery 
in overlapping window segments instead of fixed-size 
patches. When applied to small objects, this technique 
improved small-object mAP@0.50 by up to 13.2 points 
across YOLOv5, RetinaNet and Faster R-CNN. Yang et al. 
[8] designed Blade30 to stitch together drone-captured 
photographs to create full-blade views. Jia and Chen [9] 
combined optical and thermal video to produce near-real-
time segmentation. Both studies suggest that the acquisition 
and preprocessing components of the overall process are 
still being refined. Each of these studies trained their 
convolutional detectors from either scratch or from an 
ImageNet-trained backbone on a single small corpus. 
Therefore, each study addressed the resulting data shortage 
via architecture, i.e., using deeper backbones, sliced 
inference, instead of altering where the visual prior 
originated. 
Additionally, taxonomic systems developed outside the 
DTU lineage (e.g., the five-category scheme of the Liu 
dataset [10]) render different datasets incompatible with 
each other, thus limiting exploration of cross-dataset 
training. 
B. Conventional Detectors 
The baselines used for this evaluation represent the 
current single stage state-of-the-art. YOLOv8 [11], 
YOLOv9 [12] and YOLOv10 [13] further enhance the 
anchor-free, decoupled head architecture of the original 
YOLO 
architecture 
using 
programmable 
gradient 
information and NMS-free end-to- end training. RT-DETR 
[14] also uses the set prediction capabilities of DETR for 
real time inference. RT-DETR reports higher COCO 
accuracy than comparable YOLO variants. Most prior 
studies on blade inspection have benchmarked their models 
against older detection architectures. Therefore, the use of 
these newer detection architectures will provide a more 
meaningful basis for comparison. 
C. Foundation Models 
Li et al.'s [15] work shows that a simple, non-
hierarchical Vision Transformer (ViT) can serve as a 
detection backbone, provided a basic feature pyramid is 
created from a single-scale feature map. This is in contrast 
to designing a specific hierarchical network for detection. 
Therefore, the backbone does not need to be specifically 
designed for detection; it simply needs to be well pretrained, 
which is the approach followed here. Ranzinger et al. [5] 
also focused on pre-training by taking CLIP [16], DINOv2 
[17] and SAM [18] and combining them into AM-RADIO. 
AM-RADIO performs better than all three of its teachers on 
most benchmarks and also retains their zero-shot and dense-
prediction capabilities. 
However, AM-RADIO has not been evaluated for defect 
detection within an unrelated industrial domain. Eltoum et 
al.'s RadioCore [19] is the closest to evaluating AM-
RADIO. They used multi-scale, few-shot memory banks for 
anomaly segmentation on MVTec-AD and VisA without 
fine-tuning their RADIOv2.5 backbone. Training-free 
feature matching differs from fine-tuning a backbone with a 
trainable head. Bottles and circuit boards are much more 
similar to web-image pre-training data than aerial close-ups 
of composite blade surfaces are. WinCLIP [20] achieved 
91.8% AUROC on MVTec-AD by using a compositional 
prompt ensemble over CLIP embeddings, again on 
manufactured objects. Section IV-E reports a replication on 
blade imagery. Currently, there is no research comparing 
agglomerative foundation models with trainable detection 
heads for wind turbine blade defect detection to 
conventional detectors under both single-dataset and 
merged training. 
III. METHODS 
A. Datasets 
Five independently collected blade defect datasets were 
used: Blade30 [8], Gohar [6], [7], WTBs2025 [21], WTBD 
[22] and Yae; derived from Liu and Liu [10]. Each arrived 
in a different annotation format and was converted through 
a shared pipeline into YOLO-format bounding boxes under 
one six-category taxonomy (crack, erosion, lightning 
receptor damage, paint-off, missing teeth, contamination), 
so that conversion differences could not confound the 
comparison. Coverage of that taxonomy varies (Table I). 
Blade30 lacks missing-teeth instances and Gohar lacks 
contamination; Gohar's source indices were remapped as 0 
= missing teeth, 1 = erosion, 2 = lightning receptor damage, 
3 = crack, 4 = paint-off. Label correctness was verified 
across all five datasets by matching each converted image 
to its raw source and comparing derived classes against 
those written by the conversion pipeline; cross-dataset 
duplication was checked by perceptual hashing. Only one 
required exclusions; WTBs2025 [21] (referred to as ‘Zhang’ 
in this paper) provides 7,544 images across nine defect 
categories. Three categories had no equivalent in the shared 
taxonomy and were excluded. Lightning strike was 
excluded because the released data combines real 
photographs with GA-DCGAN-synthesised samples that 
could not be reliably separated. Manual review of the 
remaining categories found annotation reliability varied 
considerably: sampled crack and erosion boxes frequently 
did not contain the labelled defect, and both categories were 
excluded. Coating detachment was retained only after 
individual review of all 1,085 images, of which 161 were 
confirmed as showing the labelled defect. Contamination, 
verified as reliable in sampling, was retained in full. The 
resulting subset comprises 933 images across two

B. Model Architecture 
Two model families were compared. The conventional 
baseline comprised eighteen architectures from the YOLO 
and RT-DETR families (YOLOv8n/s/m/l , YOLOv9c/e/s/m 
, YOLOv10n/s/m/l, YOLO11n/s/m/l, RT-DETR-l/x), each 
loaded 
from 
Ultralytics' 
official 
COCO-pretrained 
checkpoints and fine-tuned separately per dataset. Starting 
both families from large-scale pretrained weights isolates 
the pretraining corpus and adaptation strategy rather than 
confounding them with the presence or absence of 
pretraining. 
The foundation-model detector pairs a fixed AM-
RADIO ViT-H/16 backbone [5] with a trainable detection 
head (Fig. 1). Spatial features are extracted at constant patch 
resolution and mapped into a three-level pyramid at strides 
8, 16 and 32 through three lightweight projection blocks, 
following the plain-backbone approach of Li et al. [15]. 
Each block applies two convolutional layers with 
GroupNorm (32 groups) and GELU activation. GroupNorm 
replaced batch normalisation because backbone memory 
limits the batch size to four, at which BatchNorm 
accumulates unstable running statistics; the training-
evaluation discrepancy reached 0.28 to 0.38 mean absolute 
difference 
before 
the 
change, 
and 
normalising 
independently of batch statistics removed it. 
A configurable number of the backbone's final 
transformer blocks are unfrozen so the pretrained 
representation can adapt, tuned as described in Section III-
D. The three pyramid levels feed a feature pyramid and path 
aggregation network built from Ultralytics' C3k2 and Conv 
modules, followed by an Ultralytics Detect head trained 
with the YOLOv8 detection loss. Sharing the neck and head 
means the two families differ in backbone and adaptation 
strategy alone, which is what the comparison isolates. 
C. Training Procedure 
The baseline models were developed using Ultralytics' 
default training loop which consists of AdamW-family 
optimisation with framework-selected configuration, batch 
sizes of 8, up to 100 epochs with early stopping after 20 
consecutive epochs without improvement, cosine learning 
rate scheduling and framework-default Exponential Moving 
Average (EMA) weight selection for validation and 
checkpointing.  
The foundation-model pipeline uses AdamW with four 
separate groups of parameters (two for the unfrozen 
backbone blocks and two for the newly initialised head). 
The decayed parameters are separated from the non-
decayed parameters. Parameters associated with bias and 
normalisation layers are excluded from weight decay. In 
addition, the learning rate for the backbone is reduced 
relative to that of the head and is discussed further in Section 
III-D. A base learning rate of 1×10⁻³ and weight decay of 
5×10⁻4 are used in conjunction with a three-epoch linear 
warm-up period followed by a cosine decay of 1% of the 
original learning rate by epoch 100. Early stopping criteria 
are identical to those of the baseline model, i.e., evaluation 
every five epochs until there has been 20 consecutive 
epochs without improvement. Additionally, Automatic 
Mixed Precision with Gradient Scaling is employed 
throughout the training process with gradient norm clipping 
at 10.0.  
An EMA of trainable parameters is employed to aid in 
model selection and checkpointing. The decay factor is 
0.9999 and the time constant is 2000 updates. These are 
Ultralytics' default values for EMA; therefore, both families 
employ similar methods to select checkpoints. As such, 
neither family is afforded an unfair advantage due to 
differing checkpoint selection rules. EMA is applied only to 
trainable parameters, as applying EMA to the frozen 
backbone will produce no benefit. 
Although both families use the same early stopping 
criteria to determine convergence, there exists an 
asymmetry between them. Ultralytics aggregates gradients 
to a nominal batch size of 64 while the foundation-model 
pipeline takes an optimiser step per batch at a batch size of 
4, representing a sixteen-fold difference in effective batch 
size. Section V examines the observed effects of this 
difference.

D. Hyperparameter Tuning 
A grid search was run per dataset over the two 
hyperparameters governing backbone adaptation: the 
number of the backbone's final transformer blocks left 
unfrozen (2, 4 or 8), controlling how much of the pretrained 
representation can change; and the backbone-to-head 
learning rate ratio (20, 50 or 100), which follows standard 
discriminative fine-tuning practice in preventing early large 
gradients from erasing useful pretrained features. This gives 
nine configurations per dataset, each trained under identical 
early-stopping criteria, with the highest EMA-selected 
validation mAP@0.50 retained. 
The remaining hyperparameters (input resolution, 
augmentation policy, scheduling length and clipping 
threshold) remained constant across all runs of the grid 
search, so that any differences observed in performance can 
be solely attributed to the two adaptation hyperparameters. 
Varying them jointly would have required a substantially 
larger search than the available compute allowed. 
Three of the five datasets, Gohar, Zhang and Yae, 
performed best with four unfrozen blocks. Yae (the largest) 
continued to show that four unfrozen blocks were preferred, 
demonstrating that adaptation depth does not increase 
proportionally with the total number of images in the 
training set. This is revisited later in Section V as it appears 
that both annotation density and class coverage matter 
alongside the sheer volume of images.  
To execute the cross-dataset experiment, the best 
performing configuration from each individual dataset's 
grid search was used as opposed to running another grid 
search because of the cost of running a single configuration 
at the scale of the combined dataset. All nine configurations 
for each dataset's grid search were trained using identical 
batch sizes, accumulation schedules, scheduling lengths and 
clipping 
thresholds; 
therefore, 
comparisons 
among 
configurations are internally controlled and not affected by 
the pipeline asymmetry described above. 
E. Vision-Language Model Investigation 
A second stream of research examined whether vision-
language models could classify blade defects based upon 
cropped portions of images; these were combined with the 
localising capabilities of the detector by providing a textual 
explanation for the identified defect. Five different prompt 
methods were used to test the ability of several general 
purpose vision-language models (BLIP-2 in both OPT and 
FlanT5-XL configurations, Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct, and a 
reproduction of the WinCLIP anomaly classifier based on 
CLIP [20]) to detect blade defects. The results of this initial 
testing are presented in Section IV-E, and they provided the 
rationale for performing the supervised fine-tuning 
discussed next.  
Fine-tuning used Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct and applied 
LoRA (low-rank adaptation, rank=16, alpha=16) to the 
vision, language, attention and MLP layers inside the 
Unsloth framework. This updated about 1.1% of the total 
number of parameters in the model (2.15 billion). The 
training dataset consisted of 6,924 labelled crops extracted 
from all five blade datasets. All crops had 50% padding 
added around the annotated bounding box, and had a 
minimum crop dimension of 100 pixels, allowing the 
surrounding blade surface to be present as contextual 
information. Additionally, 1,500 negative crops were 
generated by sampling from source image corners, giving 
the model non-defective regions to differentiate from. 
Finally, each crop was transformed into a single turn image 
and instruction pair where the ground truth class name was 
used as the desired output response.  
Training was performed for three epochs using 8-bit 
AdamW at a learning rate of 2×10-4 with linear decay and 
five warmup steps, an effective batch size of 8, and mixed 
precision. At the end of training, a final training loss of 
0.018 was achieved. Evaluation of the trained adapters was 
conducted using both the Gohar test set and WTBD 
validation split, which were kept separate from the fine-
tuning process. 
F. Ethical Considerations 
All datasets used in this study are secondary, publicly 
available or previously published research datasets 
consisting of aerial and ground-based photographs of wind 
turbine infrastructure; none contain personally identifiable 
information; no primary data collection involving human 
participants was undertaken; thus no consent procedures 
were necessary. Where datasets are derived from a shared 
underlying image source, original dataset and annotation 
provenance is credited in Section III-A and in the 
corresponding dataset descriptions above. As the foundation 
model backbone used was pretrained on broad general-
purpose web imagery unrelated to wind energy; its 
underlying training data provenance and any associated 
biases are inherited rather than independently assessed 
within this study; this is identified as a limitation in Section 
V. Given the safety-critical nature of wind turbine 
inspection, validation well beyond the scope of this study 
would be required before any of these approaches could be 
used in place of, rather than alongside, qualified human 
inspection. Fairness across defect categories is a further 
concern: pronounced class imbalance means per-class 
reliability is unequal, and the weakest classes in this study 
include structurally critical damage, so deployment would 
require per-class validation rather than aggregate accuracy.  
IV. EXPERIMENTATION AND RESULTS 
A. Hardware and Software Environment 
All experiments were conducted on Google Colab using 
a single NVIDIA A100-SXM4 GPU with 40 GB of 
memory. The software environment comprised Python 
3.13.15, PyTorch 2.11.0 with CUDA 12.8, Ultralytics 
8.4.145, and torchmetrics for detection metric computation. 
AM-RADIO ViT-H/16 checkpoints were obtained through 
torch.hub. The vision-language experiments used the 
HuggingFace transformers library with Salesforce's BLIP-2 
checkpoints, Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct, OpenCLIP with 
LAION-400M pretraining, and Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct 
fine-tuned through the Unsloth framework with low-rank 
adaptation. A full environment specification is provided in 
the supporting material. 
Conventional baselines were trained through Ultralytics' 
native pipeline with automatic mixed precision and 

framework-default augmentation. The foundation-model 
pipeline used a custom training loop implementing the 
procedure described in Section III-C, sharing the Ultralytics 
detection 
head, 
loss 
function 
and 
non-maximum 
suppression implementation so that the two families differ 
only where intended 
B. Evaluation Metrics 
Detection performance is reported as mean average 
precision at an IoU threshold of 0.50 (mAP@0.50), 
alongside mAP averaged over 0.50 to 0.95 in 0.05 
increments. The former is the standard detection metric and 
Ultralytics' internal selection criterion, allowing consistent 
comparison between families; the latter penalises imprecise 
localisation, where a plain ViT backbone with a lightweight 
projection pyramid might reasonably differ from a purpose-
designed convolutional detector. Vision-language results 
are reported as classification accuracy alongside false-
positive rate on undefected regions, since accuracy alone 
cannot distinguish genuine discrimination from a fixed bias 
toward one class. 
C. Metric Limitations 
Reporting both IoU thresholds reduces but does not 
remove the limitations of aggregate metrics. Neither value 
is a claim of deployment sufficiency, and neither reflects 
per-class reliability, which Table IV shows varies 
considerably. The WinCLIP replication in Section IV-E 
uses AUROC, matching the original publication [20], since 
a fixed threshold would misrepresent a method never 
optimised for this task. Several evaluation sets are small: 
Blade30's test split contains 93 images and some per-class 
samples number fewer than 15, so reported values carry 
meaningful sampling variance and small per-class 
differences should not be over-interpreted.

E. VLM Results 
Fine-tuning Qwen3-VL-2B via low-rank adaptation on 
6,924 domain-specific crop images yielded 57.3% 
classification accuracy on the Gohar test split and 86.5% on 
the WTBD validation split; this contrasts sharply with 
approximately 14% achieved when randomly selecting one 
of the seven possible answers. Neither split contributed to 
fine-tuning. Differences in accuracy between these two 
splits are primarily due to their different class compositions, 
specifically, WTBD has a relatively balanced distribution of 
all six categories of defects, whereas Gohar's crop images 
are heavily weighted towards two categories and have very 
few examples in some other categories.  
False positive rates for the negative crop images 
(sampled from areas where no defects were identified) were 
11.8% on Gohar and 16.0% on WTBD, showing that the 
model correctly differentiates defective blade surfaces from 
non-defective ones in most instances. This cannot be 
determined based solely upon accuracy since a model that 
consistently predicts a defect would appear successful if 
evaluated solely on a dataset containing only defects.  
Per-class results in Table IV show uneven reliability, 
from 93.1% on cracks in WTBD to 48.7% on missing teeth 
in Gohar, with the weakest categories being those with 
fewest training crops. Missing teeth is among the most 
structurally consequential defect types, which bears on the 
fairness considerations in Section III-F. Under-detection 
rather than misclassification dominates the error profile. 
Integrating the fine-tuned model with the detector 
confirms the two components function together. Using the 
detector's own predicted boxes rather than ground-truth 
annotations, detector and VLM agreed on class assignment 
in every case at detection confidence 0.22 or above. Below 
that threshold, disagreements clustered by source image 
rather than by confidence value, indicating the VLM 
responds to image content rather than defaulting to a fixed 
answer under uncertainty. 
Regarding zero-shot evaluations, it was determined that 
none of the general purpose vision-language models 
evaluated without additional training could be used for this 
domain. Both BLIP-2 configurations produced near-
identical outputs for annotated defective crops and matched 
control crops from the same source images, across all five 
prompting strategies. 
Although Qwen2.5-VL-3B exhibited a response pattern 
associated with class-based responses when using structured 
prompting techniques, altering the order of the same answer 
options for the same crop resulted in a change in the 
majority prediction from erosion (18/24) to no observable 
defect (19/25), indicating that the position of the answer 
within the list is more influential than the image content 
itself. 
 
Finally, WinCLIP was replicated using the backbones 
and ensemble of prompts outlined in the original article and 
generated an AUROC score of 0.608 relative to the 0.918 
reported in the original article's manufactured-object dataset 
and 0.500 relative to random chance. Fewer than 7,000 
domain-specific crops significantly reduced the discrepancy 
resulting from generalising pre-training alone.

The per-dataset results answer Q1 with a qualification. 
AM-RADIO 
outperformed 
the 
best 
of 
eighteen 
conventional architectures on four of five datasets, so a fine-
tuned foundation model can exceed conventional detectors 
on this task. The advantage does not hold uniformly, and 
where it fails is informative: the four successful datasets 
provided 423 to 2,314 training images, the unsuccessful one 
210. Adapting a 651-million-parameter backbone with a 
newly initialised detection head appears to require a 
minimum volume of annotated in-domain data, below 
which a smaller architecture fine-tuned from COCO 
pretraining is the better choice.

hyperparameter 
grid 
corroborates 
this 
independently. Adaptation was most conservative on 
Blade30, at two unfrozen blocks, so the dataset requiring 
least backbone modification is also the one where the 
approach failed. That two independent lines of evidence 
point to the same threshold strengthens the reading, since 
the grid was not constructed to test it. 
The threshold is not a simple function of image count: 
adaptation depth and margin size both track annotation 
density and class coverage as much as they track dataset 
size.

directly to aerial industrial images without additional 
training data, a relatively small amount of domain-specific 
training data can enable the model to achieve comparable 
results. 
A. Limitations 
 
Some limitations restrict the generalisability of 
these findings. As Ultralytics accumulates gradients to an 
effective batch of 64 and the foundation model pipeline 
updates gradient per batch of 4, the effective batch sizes for 
each of the two pipelines differ by a factor of 16. On nearly 
all iterations, gradient norm clipping at 10.0 occurred, with 
pre-clip norms varying from approximately 100 to 500. 
Clipping effectively limits per-step updates regardless of 
batch size; thus, the effects of the differences in batch size 
are greatly reduced. Both families of models were trained 
until convergence using the same early stopping criterion. 
Therefore, although the use of early stopping does not 
completely eliminate the possibility of bias due to training 
time, it substantially reduces it.  
 
Additionally, WTBD does not have a separate test set, 
therefore the validation set provided by WTBD was used for 
evaluation purposes for all comparisons. As such, the 
WTBD results are less rigorously held out from testing than 
those obtained from the other four datasets. 
 
The merged baseline was YOLOv9s alone rather than 
the best of the eighteen-architecture sweep used per dataset. 
Results are also reported from single training runs; 
checkpoint-to-checkpoint variation reached 0.15 mAP on 
the smaller datasets, so the Blade30 margin should be read 
as indicative rather than established. 
VI. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 
This work evaluated whether a large-scale visual 
foundation model, fine-tuned with a lightweight detection 
head, outperforms conventional detectors on wind turbine 
blade defect detection. AM-RADIO was compared against 
eighteen 
conventional 
architectures 
across 
five 
independently collected datasets, first per dataset and then 
under merged training with Blade30 held out entirely. Both 
families started from large-scale pretrained weights and 
were trained to convergence under identical criteria. 
AM-RADIO exceeded the strongest conventional 
baseline on four of five datasets, by 0.0218 to 0.0704 
mAP@0.50, and underperformed by 0.0907 on the smallest. 
The failure case is the substantive finding: foundation-
model adaptation in this domain appears to require a 
minimum volume of annotated in-domain data, on the order 
of several hundred images, below which a smaller 
architecture fine-tuned from general pretraining is the better 
choice. 
The 
hyperparameter 
search 
supports 
this 
independently, since the dataset requiring the most 
conservative backbone adaptation is the one where the 
approach failed. 
A parallel investigation found that general-purpose 
vision-language models do not transfer to this domain zero-
shot, the best replication reaching 0.608 AUROC against 
0.500 for chance, but that low-rank fine-tuning of Qwen3-
VL-2B on fewer than 7,000 domain crops reached 86.5% 
classification accuracy on held-out data. 
Several questions remain open. It is unclear if the 
threshold identified is unique to AM-RADIO and its 
adaptation methodology, or if it represents a common 
characteristic of all foundation model fine-tuning for 
detection-type tasks. Only one backbone was evaluated. 
Similarly, it is unclear why the adaptation depth did not 
increase linearly as the size of the dataset increased. 
Annotation density or class composition may affect how 
optimal adaptation strategies are determined in ways that 
were not studied in this research.
